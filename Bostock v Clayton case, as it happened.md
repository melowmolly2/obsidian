In the landmark decision of Bostock v Clayton County, the Supreme court of the United States ruled 6-3 that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 protects employees from being discharged or discriminated on these grounds requires an employer to intentionally treat an individual differently at least in part because of their sex, such actions fall under the statutory prohibition against discrimination "because of ... sex."
The decision resolved a circuit split across three cases involving long-time employees who were fired shortly after revealing they were homosexual or transgender. The Majority Opinion, authored by Justice Gorsuch, relied on a textualist interpretation of the law, applying a "but-for" causation standard. The Dissenting Opinions, led by Justice Alito, argued that the Court had usurped legislative authority by "updating" the 1964 statute to reflect contemporary social values, warning of significant downstream consequences for religious liberty, personal liberty, personal privacy, and women's sports. 

## Background and Consolidated Cases:
- **Bostock v. Clayton County (Gerald Bostock)**: fired for conduct "unbecoming" a county employee after joining a gay recreational softball league.
- **Altitude Express, Inc. v. Zarda (Donald Zarda)**: Fired days after mentioning he was gay.
- **R.G. & G.R. Harris Funeral Homes v. EEOC (Aimee Stephens)**: Fired after informing the employer she planned to "live and work full-time as a woman." 
In the lower courts, the Eleventh Circuits held that Title VII did not prohibit firing employees for being gay, while the Second and Sixth Circuits ruled that the law did protect homosexual and transgender employees, respectively. 

## The Majority Opinion:
The Court's decision rests on the "ordinary public meaning" of the terms in Title VII as they were written in 1964: 
1. **The Definitions of Key Statutory Terms (by the court's definition)**:
	The court analyzed 42 U.S.C. §2000e–2(a)(1) which forbids discrimination 'because of" an individual's "sex."
	- Sex: refers to the biological distinctions between male and female
	- Because of: incorporates the "but-for" causation standard. Liability is triggered if an outcome (the firing) would not have happened but for the protected trait. 
	- Discriminate: make a difference in treatment or favor. 
	- Individual: The statute focuses on "any individual", meaning the law protects a particular being as distinguished from a class or group. 
2. **The "But-For" Causation Rule**: 
	The Court established that an employer violates title VII if they intentionally rely in part on an individual employee's sex. 
	- Multiple Causes: It is irrelevant if other factors (such as sexual orientation) contributed to the decision. If sex is one but-for causes, the employer is liable. 
	- Comparative test: If an employer fires a male employee for being attracted to men but retains a female employee attracted to men, the employer has discriminated against the man for a trait tolerated in a woman. The employee's sex is an "undisguisable role" in that decision. 
3. **Individual vs. Group Protections**. 
	 The Court rejected the defense that an employer who treats men and women as _groups_ equally (e.g., firing both gay men and gay women) is exempt from liability. Because Title VII protects the _individual_, an employer who fires a woman for being gay and a man for being gay has doubled their liability rather than eliminated it, as they have discriminated against two individuals based on their sex.
4. **Text vs Legislative Anticipation**
	  The Majority argued that the limits of the drafters' imaginations in 1964 do not restrict the law's application. Even if the legislators did not anticipate this specific result, the "written word is the law." The Court noted that "sexual harassment" and "motherhood" were also not the principal concerns of the 1964 Congress, yet both are now clearly covered under Title VII’s sex provision.

## Judicidal Precedents Cited
The Majority used three leading precedents to confirm their interpretation: 
- **Phillips v. Martin Marietta Corp. (1971):** Held that a company could not refuse to hire women with young children even if they hired women as a class and the discrimination involved a non-protected trait (motherhood).
- **Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power v. Manhart (1978):** Ruled that requiring women to make larger pension contributions because they live longer was discriminatory, even if the policy was based on accurate actuarial tables.
- **Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc. (1998):** Confirmed that Title VII protects men from sexual harassment by other men, noting that statutory prohibitions often go beyond the "principal evil" legislators intended to address.

## Dissenting Perspective: Justice Alito and Justice Thomas
Justice Alito's dissent criticized the majority for "legislating" under the guise of statutory interpretation. 
1. **Violation of Constitutional Authority**
	The dissent argued that because neither "sexual orientation" nor "gender identity" appears in the text of Title VII, and because Congress has repeatedly failed to pass bills adding these categories over the last 45 years, the Court essentially bypassed the legislative process. 
2. **Historical Social Context**
	In 1964, the "ordinary public meaning" of discrimination because of sex would not have been understood to include sexual orientation or gender identity. 
	 - Social Norms: At the time, homosexuality was classified as a mental disorder by the APA and homosexual conduct was a crime in almost every state. 
	 - Federal Policy: In 1964, the federal government itself denied security clearances to homosexual individuals and the military barred their services. It is "preposterous" to suggest the 1964 public thought they were outlawing private sector practices that were official government policy. 
3. Distinct Concepts Argument
	The dissent argued that an employer could have a blanket policy against hiring any gay or transgender person while remaining ignorant of their biological sex (e.g. through a blind application process). Therefore, sexual orientation and gender identity are conceptually distinct from biological sex, and discrimination against the former does not inherently involve the latter. 
### Potential Downstream Consequences
Justice Alito warned that the Court's reasoning would "mire" the federal judiciary in disputes for years regarding other laws that prohibit sex discrimination (e.g. Title IX,  the Fair Housing Act, and the Affordable Care Act) .
- **Privacy and Safety:** Challenges to sex-segregated bathrooms and locker rooms by transgender or "genderfluid" individuals. 
- **Women's Sports:** Potential for biological males who identify as female to compete in women's sports, threatening the achievements of Title IX.
- **Religious Liberty**: Potential conflicts between Title VII and the practices of religious organizations that require employees to adhere to specific faith-based conduct. 
- **Healthcare:** Requirements for employers or healthcare providers to cover or perform sex reassignment procedures despite religious objections. 
- **Freedom of speech**: Pressure on employers or teachers to use preferred pronouns or suppress religious views regarding same-sex relationships. 
## Definitions of "Sex" in 1964:
The Dissent provided exhaustive dictionary definitions from the era of Title VII's enactment to prove that "sex" referred only to biological division: 
 - Webster's New International (1953): "One of the two divisions of organisms formed on the distinction of male and female." 
 - American Heritage (1969): "The property or quality by which organisms are classified according to their reproductive functions."
 - Oxford English Dictionary (1933): "Either of the two divisions of organic beings distinguished as male and female respectively."

**Why Justice Alito argues his position is correct:**

- **Judicial Overreach:** Alito contends that the majority is effectively writing new legislation from the bench rather than interpreting the law, updating a 1964 statute to reflect 2020 values. He characterizes the majority opinion as a "pirate ship" that sails under a textualist flag but actually abandons textualism to achieve a desired social outcome.
- **Original Public Meaning:** Alito argues that a statute's words must mean what they conveyed to reasonable people at the time they were written. In 1964, dictionaries defined "sex" strictly as the biological and anatomical differences between males and females. At that time, "gender identity" was an essentially unknown concept, and homosexuality was widely criminalized and classified as a mental disorder. Consequently, he argues it is impossible that the 1964 public or Congress understood Title VII to protect these categories.
- **Distinct Concepts:** He argues that sexual orientation and transgender status are entirely distinct from biological sex. To prove this, he points out that an employer could institute a blanket ban on hiring gay or transgender individuals without ever knowing a specific applicant's biological sex. If an employer can discriminate on these bases without knowing an employee's sex, Alito reasons, then it is not inherently "sex discrimination".
- **Unintended Consequences:** Alito warns that the majority's reasoning is irresponsible and will have massive ripple effects across over 100 federal statutes. He argues it will threaten women's sports by forcing biological females to compete against biological males, disrupt sex-segregated bathrooms and locker rooms, and trigger intense conflicts over the employment practices of religious organizations.

**Why the Majority (Justice Gorsuch) ruled against him:**

- **The "But-For" Causation Test:** The majority argues that it is logically impossible to fire someone for being homosexual or transgender without their biological sex playing a necessary role. Using the "but-for" test, the Court explains that if an employer fires a male employee for being attracted to men, but would not fire a female employee for being attracted to men, the employer is penalizing the man for a trait it tolerates in a woman. Therefore, sex is an inescapable factor in the firing.
- **Text Over Expectations:** The majority concedes that the 1964 Congress likely did not anticipate that the Civil Rights Act would protect gay or transgender individuals. However, Gorsuch argues that the limits of the drafters' imaginations do not provide a reason to ignore the law's explicit demands. When the plain, written words of a statute demand a certain outcome, those words overrule extratextual considerations like the original expectations or "principal concerns" of the legislators.
- **Focus on the Individual:** While Alito argues that employers might treat gay men and gay women equally poorly, the majority notes that Title VII explicitly protects "any individual". An employer cannot defend themselves by claiming they fire gay men and gay women equally; doing so simply means they are doubling their liability by discriminating against individuals of both sexes.

Ultimately, determining whether Alito's dissent is "correct" depends on whether you believe a law should be interpreted based on the original public meaning, societal norms, and congressional intent at the time of enactment (Justice Alito's view) or by the strict, logical application of the statute's plain text to individual cases (Justice Gorsuch's view).
